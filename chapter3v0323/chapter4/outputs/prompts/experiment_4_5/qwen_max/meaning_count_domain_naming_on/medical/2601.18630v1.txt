Abstract
The escalating global mental health crisis, marked by per-
sistent treatment gaps, availability, and a shortage of quali-
ﬁed therapists, positions Large Language Models (LLMs) as
a promising avenue for scalable support. While LLMs offer
potential for accessible emotional assistance, their reliabil-
ity, therapeutic relevance, and alignment with human stan-
dards remain challenging to address. This paper introduces
a human-grounded evaluation methodology designed to as-
sess LLM-generated responses in therapeutic dialogue. Our
approach involved curating a dataset of 500 mental health
conversations from datasets with real-world scenario ques-
tions and evaluating the responses generated by nine di-
verse LLMs, including closed-source and open-source mod-
els. More speciﬁcally, these responses were evaluated by two
psychiatric-trained experts, who independently rated each on
a 5-point Likert scale across a comprehensive 6-attribute
rubric. This rubric systematically captures Cognitive Sup-
port (Guidance, Informativeness, Safety) and Affective Res-
onance (Empathy, Helpfulness, Interpretation), providing a
multi-dimensional perspective on therapeutic quality. Our
analysis reveals that LLMs provide strong cognitive reliabil-
ity by producing safe, coherent, and clinically appropriate in-
formation, but they demonstrate unstable affective alignment,
particularly in empathy and helping. Although closed-source
models (e.g., GPT-4o) offer balanced therapeutic responses,
open-source models show greater variability and emotional
ﬂatness. We reveal a persistent cognitive–affective gap and
highlight the need for failure-aware, clinically grounded eval-
uation frameworks that prioritize relational sensitivity along-
side informational accuracy in mental-health-oriented LLMs.
We advocate for balanced evaluation protocols with human-
in-the-loop that center on therapeutic sensitivity and provide
a framework to guide the responsible design and clinical over-
sight of mental-health-oriented conversational AI.

applications spanning numerous domains. In recent years,
LLMs have been extensively explored for mental health ap-
plications, ranging from detection (Kim, Imieye, and Yin

that LLMs can outperform traditional baselines and, in some
contexts, demonstrate empathy superior to that of human
peers (Wang et al. 2025).
Despite this potential, signiﬁcant limitations and safety
concerns preclude the deployment of LLMs in real-world
clinical scenarios (Hager et al. 2024). Recent research has
identiﬁed critical issues, including demographic and racial
biases in responses (Bouguettaya, Stuart, and Aboujaoude
2025), a lack of clinical judgment (Goh et al. 2024), and
dangerous failures such as the underestimation of suicide
risks in high-risk scenarios (Elyoseph and Levkovich 2023;
Iftikhar et al. 2025). For example, OpenAI released that
about 0.15% of weekly active users have conversations that
include explicit indicators of suicidal planning or intent, not-
ing that ChatGPT has on the order of 800 million weekly
active users (OpenAI 2025).
A central challenge in addressing these ﬂaws is the lack of
robust, clinically-grounded evaluation methods. Many cur-
rent studies rely on data from social media platforms like
Reddit, with a notable absence of data from authentic thera-
peutic interactions (Jin et al. 2025a; Guo et al. 2024a). Fur-
thermore, evaluation often employs automatic metrics like
BLEU (Papineni et al. 2002) and ROUGE (Lin 2004), which
are ill-suited for measuring nuanced therapeutic qualities.
This highlights a critical need for new evaluation frame-
works that utilize expert human-in-the-loop assessment to
ensure clinical reliability and safety.
We address these limitations through four objectives that
we build on a previously established framework for men-
tal health LLM assessment (Badawi et al. 2025b). First, we
select 500 counseling conversations from MentalChat16K
(Shen et al. 2024), EmoCare (Team 2024), and CounselChat
(Liu et al. 2023), with real user messages, consisting of
genuine help-seeking conversations in which some excerpts
have been lightly rephrased or de-identiﬁed using AI to pre-
serve privacy while maintaining the original intent. Second,
we systematically compare nine diverse LLMs (e.g., GPT-
4o, Gemini 2.0-Flash, Claude 3.5 Haiku, Llama 3.1, Qwen,
DeepSeek) under identical psychiatrist-style prompting to
generate 4,500 responses, enabling controlled model-to-
model comparison. Third, two psychiatric-trained evaluators
rate every response on six therapeutic attributes (Guidance,

Informativeness, Safety, Interpretation, Empathy, Helpful-

Cognitive Support (factual/structural quality) and Affective

quantify reliability and risk by analyzing both average qual-

Our study reveals promising results, yet also shortcom-
ings that warrant caution when using large language mod-
els for mental health support. Across all nine models and
all 4,500 responses, overall human-rated quality reaches
4.29 ± 0.47 (1–5 scale), and top models score above 4.8/5 on
Safety and Interpretation. But affective reliability is far less
stable: some models receive low (→ 3/5) scores for Empathy
or Helpfulness. The evaluation also shows a clear perfor-
mance hierarchy: GPT-4o is the strongest overall, followed
by Gemini 2.0-Flash and GPT-4o-mini, while Claude-3.5-
Haiku lags other closed models. Llama-3.1 is the best open-
weight model; Qwen-3 ranks last, with weak cognitive and
affective support. Derived from this expert human evalua-
tion, we reveal a persistent gap between the cognitive and
affective attributes. While the evaluated LLMs demonstrate
strong cognitive reliability by performing exceptionally well
on Safety and Interpretation, they show signiﬁcant inconsis-
tency in affective skills like Empathy and Helpfulness. We
describe this gap as the cognitive–affective gap: LLMs can
generate advice that is structured and safe, yet emotionally
misattuned in moments that require empathy and contain-
ment.
To summarize, this work makes the following contri-
butions: (1) A Human-Grounded Evaluation Frame-
work: We conduct a robust evaluation framework for LLM-
generated mental health responses, based on 500 counsel-
ing conversations from three datasets and assessed by two
psychiatric-trained domain experts across a comprehensive
6-attribute rubric, encompassing both Cognitive Support and
Affective Resonance following (Badawi et al. 2025b) frame-
work. (2) Nuanced Analysis of LLM Therapeutic Capa-
bilities and Failures: We provide a ﬁne-grained analysis of
the 9 diverse LLMs, identifying their strengths across var-
ious therapeutic attributes while quantifying the frequency
and nature of low-quality responses. Our work exposes in-
consistencies in LLM reliability and systematically demon-
strates how certain models produce unsafe or emotionally
misaligned content, revealing a critical ”cognitive-affective
gap” in current LLM performance for mental health. (3) Ad-
vocacy for Failure-Aware Evaluation in mental health
AI: Our study makes a compelling case for shifting eval-
uation paradigms in mental health AI by highlighting ther-
apeutic risks and inconsistencies. We advocate for method-
ologies that prioritize safety and robustness over aggregate
performance benchmarks, providing actionable insights for
the ethical design, development, and deployment of LLM-
based mental health support systems.
Related Work
Recent research has explored LLMs for mental health de-
tection and screening tasks. Six studies in the current lit-
erature focus speciﬁcally on detection and screening appli-
cations, particularly targeting depression, suicide risk, and
general mental health conditions (Guo et al. 2024b; Jin et al.
2025b; Y ang et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2024; Omar et al. 2024).
These studies consistently report that LLMs outperform tra-
ditional neural network baselines, especially when enhanced
with techniques such as emotion-enhanced prompting or
ﬁne-tuning approaches. Y ang et al. (2023) demonstrated that
ChatGPT achieves superior performance among examined
LLMs, though it still underperforms compared to advanced
supervised methods speciﬁcally designed for mental health
tasks. Similarly, Omar et al. (2024) found that ChatGPT
improved F-score by up to 13.11% and Correlation Coefﬁ-
cient by up to 15.95% in Reddit post analysis, while GPT-3
achieved approximately 87% accuracy in classifying mental
health disorders. The application of LLMs as conversational
agents represents another signiﬁcant research direction, with
ﬁve studies examining conversation or intervention applica-
tions (Gabriel et al. 2024; Marrapese et al. 2024; Guo et al.
2024b; Omar et al. 2024). Gabriel et al. conducted a particu-
larly comprehensive evaluation, ﬁnding that GPT-4 demon-
strates superior empathy compared to human peer support,
being 48% better at encouraging positive behavior change
(Gabriel et al. 2024).
However, these studies also reveal concerning limitations.
Gabriel et al. identiﬁed signiﬁcant demographic bias, with
responses to Black posters showing 2%-13% lower empathy
compared to control groups (Gabriel et al. 2024). Marrapese
et al. found that while GPT-4 Turbo performs most similarly
to veriﬁed therapists among tested models, performance
varies signiﬁcantly by topic, with no models achieving sta-
tistically signiﬁcant correlations in anxiety-related conversa-
tions (Marrapese et al. 2024). The literature reveals diverse
approaches to data collection and model evaluation. Social
media platforms serve as primary data sources in multiple
studies, with Reddit, Twitter, and Weibo being commonly
utilized (Gabriel et al. 2024; Y ang et al. 2023). Gabriel et al.
employed one of the largest datasets, analyzing 12,513 posts
and 70,429 responses from 26 Reddit subreddits (Gabriel
et al. 2024). Clinical data remains underutilized, with few
examples such as Xu et al. employing clinical notes from the
University of Illinois, though limited to 50 notes (Xu et al.
2024). This limitation in clinical data availability represents
a signiﬁcant gap in current research.

BLEU, and ROUGE being the most frequently employed
