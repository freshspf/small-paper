Conclusion and Future Work
This study presents a human-grounded evaluation of nine
large language models (LLMs) across 500 counseling con-
versations. Most LLMs demonstrate strong cognitive reli-
ability—especially inSafety,Interpretation, andInforma-
tiveness—but show inconsistent affective skills inEmpathy
andHelpfulness. This persistent asymmetry between factualprecision and emotional attunement limits their therapeutic
validity. Closed-source models such as GPT-4o and Gem-
ini 2.0-Flash consistently outperform open-source models,
achieving over 90% high-confidence ratings with the lowest
incidence of low-scoring responses. Yet even top-tier sys-
tems exhibit subtle failures in emotional resonance, indi-
cating that advances in conversational safety have not been
matched by equivalent progress in affective alignment.
Our findings highlight that the core limitation of current
mental-health LLMs is not overt harm but a lack of con-
sistent emotional calibration—responses that are accurate
and safe yet fail to convey warmth or relational understand-
ing. This “cognitive-affective gap” underscores the need for
multi-dimensional fine-tuning strategies that jointly opti-
mize informational accuracy and interpersonal sensitivity.
The multi-attribute human evaluation framework introduced
here provides a transparent foundation for benchmarking
therapeutic behaviors and advancing standardized assess-
ment in digital mental health research. It also supports re-
framing LLMs from mere assistants to potentialco-creators
in care, provided their design and deployment remain ethi-
cally grounded and clinically supervised. Future work will
extend this evaluation to multi-turn dialogues, diverse cul-
tural contexts, and larger pools of expert raters. We also aim
to identify specific failure modes driving affective inconsis-
tency and to develop automated metrics that align with ex-
pert human judgment, enabling scalable yet clinically valid
assessment of mental-health-oriented AI systems.
Ethical Statement
This work involves sensitive mental health data and AI-
generated responses, warranting careful ethical reflection.
The models evaluated in this study are not intended to re-
place human therapists, and there remains a significant risk
that users may misinterpret or overly rely on AI-generated
responses (Badawi et al. 2025a). Moreover, LLMs can ex-
hibit demographic and cultural biases that may compromise
fairness, particularly when applied to diverse populations
(Obadinma et al. 2025). The optimization for structured met-
rics may overlook deeper therapeutic nuances, and the po-
tential emotional burden on human annotators reviewing dis-
tressing content was acknowledged and addressed. Future
work must prioritize explainability, real-world validation,
and ongoing oversight to ensure ethical deployment of AI

in mental health settings (Badawi et al. 2025b). Moreover,
for human evaluation, no additional compensations were re-
quired since it was conducted by the co-authors of this paper.
