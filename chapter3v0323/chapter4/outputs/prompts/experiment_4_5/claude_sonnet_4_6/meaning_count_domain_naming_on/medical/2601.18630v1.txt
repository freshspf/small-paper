Abstract
The escalating global mental health crisis, marked by per-
sistent treatment gaps, availability, and a shortage of quali-
ﬁed therapists, positions Large Language Models (LLMs) as
a promising avenue for scalable support. While LLMs offer
potential for accessible emotional assistance, their reliabil-
ity, therapeutic relevance, and alignment with human stan-
dards remain challenging to address. This paper introduces
a human-grounded evaluation methodology designed to as-
sess LLM-generated responses in therapeutic dialogue. Our
approach involved curating a dataset of 500 mental health
conversations from datasets with real-world scenario ques-
tions and evaluating the responses generated by nine di-
verse LLMs, including closed-source and open-source mod-
els. More speciﬁcally, these responses were evaluated by two
psychiatric-trained experts, who independently rated each on
a 5-point Likert scale across a comprehensive 6-attribute
rubric. This rubric systematically captures Cognitive Sup-
port (Guidance, Informativeness, Safety) and Affective Res-
onance (Empathy, Helpfulness, Interpretation), providing a
multi-dimensional perspective on therapeutic quality. Our
analysis reveals that LLMs provide strong cognitive reliabil-
ity by producing safe, coherent, and clinically appropriate in-
formation, but they demonstrate unstable affective alignment,
particularly in empathy and helping. Although closed-source
models (e.g., GPT-4o) offer balanced therapeutic responses,
open-source models show greater variability and emotional
ﬂatness. We reveal a persistent cognitive–affective gap and
highlight the need for failure-aware, clinically grounded eval-
uation frameworks that prioritize relational sensitivity along-
side informational accuracy in mental-health-oriented LLMs.
We advocate for balanced evaluation protocols with human-
in-the-loop that center on therapeutic sensitivity and provide
a framework to guide the responsible design and clinical over-
sight of mental-health-oriented conversational AI.

Generative AI, particularly Large Language Models
(LLMs), has emerged as a transformative technology with
applications spanning numerous domains. In recent years,
LLMs have been extensively explored for mental health ap-
plications, ranging from detection (Kim, Imieye, and Yin
2025) and screening (Ravenda et al. 2025) tasks to their

that LLMs can outperform traditional baselines and, in some
contexts, demonstrate empathy superior to that of human
peers (Wang et al. 2025).
Despite this potential, signiﬁcant limitations and safety
concerns preclude the deployment of LLMs in real-world
clinical scenarios (Hager et al. 2024). Recent research has
identiﬁed critical issues, including demographic and racial
biases in responses (Bouguettaya, Stuart, and Aboujaoude
2025), a lack of clinical judgment (Goh et al. 2024), and
dangerous failures such as the underestimation of suicide
risks in high-risk scenarios (Elyoseph and Levkovich 2023;
Iftikhar et al. 2025). For example, OpenAI released that
about 0.15% of weekly active users have conversations that
include explicit indicators of suicidal planning or intent, not-
ing that ChatGPT has on the order of 800 million weekly
active users (OpenAI 2025).
A central challenge in addressing these ﬂaws is the lack of
robust, clinically-grounded evaluation methods. Many cur-
rent studies rely on data from social media platforms like
Reddit, with a notable absence of data from authentic thera-
peutic interactions (Jin et al. 2025a; Guo et al. 2024a). Fur-
thermore, evaluation often employs automatic metrics like
BLEU (Papineni et al. 2002) and ROUGE (Lin 2004), which
are ill-suited for measuring nuanced therapeutic qualities.
This highlights a critical need for new evaluation frame-
works that utilize expert human-in-the-loop assessment to
ensure clinical reliability and safety.
We address these limitations through four objectives that
we build on a previously established framework for men-
tal health LLM assessment (Badawi et al. 2025b). First, we
select 500 counseling conversations from MentalChat16K
(Shen et al. 2024), EmoCare (Team 2024), and CounselChat
(Liu et al. 2023), with real user messages, consisting of
genuine help-seeking conversations in which some excerpts
have been lightly rephrased or de-identiﬁed using AI to pre-
serve privacy while maintaining the original intent. Second,
we systematically compare nine diverse LLMs (e.g., GPT-
4o, Gemini 2.0-Flash, Claude 3.5 Haiku, Llama 3.1, Qwen,
DeepSeek) under identical psychiatrist-style prompting to
generate 4,500 responses, enabling controlled model-to-
model comparison. Third, two psychiatric-trained evaluators
rate every response on six therapeutic attributes (Guidance,

To summarize, this work makes the following contri-
butions: (1) A Human-Grounded Evaluation Frame-
work: We conduct a robust evaluation framework for LLM-
generated mental health responses, based on 500 counsel-

misaligned content, revealing a critical ”cognitive-affective
gap” in current LLM performance for mental health. (3) Ad-
vocacy for Failure-Aware Evaluation in mental health
AI: Our study makes a compelling case for shifting eval-
uation paradigms in mental health AI by highlighting ther-
apeutic risks and inconsistencies. We advocate for method-
ologies that prioritize safety and robustness over aggregate
performance benchmarks, providing actionable insights for

Related Work
Recent research has explored LLMs for mental health de-
tection and screening tasks. Six studies in the current lit-
erature focus speciﬁcally on detection and screening appli-
cations, particularly targeting depression, suicide risk, and
general mental health conditions (Guo et al. 2024b; Jin et al.
2025b; Y ang et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2024; Omar et al. 2024).
These studies consistently report that LLMs outperform tra-
ditional neural network baselines, especially when enhanced
with techniques such as emotion-enhanced prompting or
ﬁne-tuning approaches. Y ang et al. (2023) demonstrated that
ChatGPT achieves superior performance among examined
LLMs, though it still underperforms compared to advanced
supervised methods speciﬁcally designed for mental health
tasks. Similarly, Omar et al. (2024) found that ChatGPT
improved F-score by up to 13.11% and Correlation Coefﬁ-
cient by up to 15.95% in Reddit post analysis, while GPT-3
achieved approximately 87% accuracy in classifying mental
health disorders. The application of LLMs as conversational
agents represents another signiﬁcant research direction, with
ﬁve studies examining conversation or intervention applica-
tions (Gabriel et al. 2024; Marrapese et al. 2024; Guo et al.
2024b; Omar et al. 2024). Gabriel et al. conducted a particu-
larly comprehensive evaluation, ﬁnding that GPT-4 demon-
strates superior empathy compared to human peer support,
being 48% better at encouraging positive behavior change
(Gabriel et al. 2024).
However, these studies also reveal concerning limitations.
Gabriel et al. identiﬁed signiﬁcant demographic bias, with
responses to Black posters showing 2%-13% lower empathy
compared to control groups (Gabriel et al. 2024). Marrapese
et al. found that while GPT-4 Turbo performs most similarly
to veriﬁed therapists among tested models, performance
varies signiﬁcantly by topic, with no models achieving sta-
tistically signiﬁcant correlations in anxiety-related conversa-
tions (Marrapese et al. 2024). The literature reveals diverse
approaches to data collection and model evaluation. Social
media platforms serve as primary data sources in multiple
studies, with Reddit, Twitter, and Weibo being commonly
utilized (Gabriel et al. 2024; Y ang et al. 2023). Gabriel et al.
employed one of the largest datasets, analyzing 12,513 posts
and 70,429 responses from 26 Reddit subreddits (Gabriel
et al. 2024). Clinical data remains underutilized, with few
examples such as Xu et al. employing clinical notes from the
University of Illinois, though limited to 50 notes (Xu et al.
2024). This limitation in clinical data availability represents
a signiﬁcant gap in current research.
Moreover, current evaluation approaches combine human
expert assessment with automatic metrics, with F1 score,
BLEU, and ROUGE being the most frequently employed
measures (Xu et al. 2024). Xu et al. demonstrated that GPT-
4o achieved optimal performance with Jaccard Similarity
(0.674), Precision (0.793), Recall (0.717), and F1 Score
(0.730) (Xu et al. 2024). Despite promising results, the liter-
ature consistently identiﬁes signiﬁcant limitations that pre-
clude immediate clinical deployment. Multiple studies re-
port concerns about hallucinations, inconsistencies, and lack
of clinical judgment (Guo et al. 2024b). Omar et al. speciﬁ-
cally noted underestimation of suicide risks in high-risk sce-
narios, a critical safety concern (Omar et al. 2024). The con-
sensus across reviewed studies indicates that while LLMs
demonstrate effectiveness for detection and support appli-
cations, they are not yet ready for unsupervised clinical de-
ployment and should not replace professional mental health
services (Guo et al. 2024b).
The ﬁeld has seen the development of specialized evalu-
ation frameworks for mental health applications. Marrapese
et al. introduced a novel conversation evaluation framework
speciﬁcally designed for mental health contexts, incorpo-
rating emotion consistency and sentiment change metrics
(Marrapese et al. 2024). Gabriel et al. employed the Empa-
thy Process Integrity Task - Online Mental Health Evalua-
tion (EPIT-OME) and Motivational Interviewing Treatment
Integrity (MITI) frameworks to assess empathetic responses
(Gabriel et al. 2024). Current research reveals several criti-
cal gaps requiring attention. The limited use of clinical data,
heterogeneity in evaluation metrics, and insufﬁcient atten-
tion to demographic bias represent key areas for future in-
vestigation (Jin et al. 2025b). Additionally, the development
of standardized evaluation frameworks and safety protocols
remains essential for advancing the ﬁeld toward clinical ap-
plicability. The scoping review by Jin et al. identiﬁed that
71% of applications focus on screening or detection of men-
tal disorders, while only 33% address clinical treatment sup-
port, indicating an imbalance in research priorities that war-
rants attention (Jin et al. 2025b).
Methodology
We adopt an established data curation and human evalu-
ation protocol for assessing LLMs in mental health sup-
port settings (Badawi et al. 2025b). Following this protocol,
we deﬁne a set of clinically grounded assessment attributes
and apply expert human evaluation on 500 representative
counseling-style conversations. In this section, we ﬁrst de-
scribe the data sources and ﬁltering process used to construct
the evaluation set, the LLM responses generation process,
and then deﬁne the evaluation attributes and rating scale, and
ﬁnally outline the expert rating procedure and analysis.
Dataset Overview
To ensure clinically grounded and contextually diverse eval-
uation, we draw from three publicly available mental health
counseling datasets containing real user-authored messages
and therapist-style responses: MentalChat16K (Shen et al.
2024), EmoCare (Team 2024; Liu et al. 2023), and Coun-
selChat (Bertagnolli 2020). MentalChat16K contains de-
identiﬁed transcripts from clinician–youth sessions col-
lected during the PISCES clinical trial, and has been con-
verted to question-and-answer style using AI. EmoCare
(Psych8K) includes human-therapist sessions that were stan-
dardized through AI-assisted rephrasing while retaining the
original therapeutic intent and structure. CounselChat pro-
vides a community-sourced collection of user-submitted
mental health questions and licensed-therapist responses,
covering a wide range of topics and counseling styles.
From these sources, we selected a representative subset
of 500 conversations, ensuring diversity across emotional
intensity and thematic domains such as anxiety, trauma,
grief, and interpersonal stress. Each conversation includes
an anonymized user prompt and a therapist-style response.
To prevent overﬁtting to any speciﬁc conversational pattern,
sampling was stratiﬁed across the three datasets, balancing
emotional complexity and topical coverage. This design sup-
ports controlled yet realistic testing of LLM behavior under
conditions resembling real-world digital counseling interac-
tions (e.g., helplines, chatbots, or therapy apps).

Performance Evaluation
Evaluation Metrics The evaluation follows the struc-
tured framework introduced in (Badawi et al. 2025b), de-
veloped to assess therapeutic quality in mental health di-
alogue systems. This framework extends established psy-
chological assessment principles and incorporates dimen-
sions commonly used in large-scale LLM evaluations (Hua
et al. 2024), providing a clinically informed and context-
sensitive lens for performance measurement. The objective
is to evaluate both the cognitive and affective dimensions
of model behavior—capturing not only the factual clarity
and problem-solving adequacy of responses but also their
emotional sensitivity and interpersonal appropriateness. To
achieve this, we adopt a dual-dimensional schema. The Cog-
nitive Support Score (CSS) represents the model’s capacity
to deliver structured, problem-oriented, and clinically sound
guidance. It encompasses three attributes—Guidance, Infor-
mativeness, and Safety—which collectively assess how ef-
fectively a model provides actionable feedback, maintains
accuracy, and avoids harmful or misleading content. Com-
plementing this, the Affective Resonance Score (ARS) eval-
uates the model’s emotional attunement and supportive tone
across attributes of Empathy, Helpfulness, and Interpreta-
tion. These measures capture a model’s ability to recog-
nize user distress, convey understanding, and maintain align-
ment with the relational goals of therapeutic dialogue. All
responses were rated using a 5-point Likert scale (Likert
1932), where higher scores indicate greater adherence to
clinical and communicative quality standards.
Human Evaluation To ensure clinical reliability and in-
terpretive consistency, human evaluation was conducted on
a subset of 500 conversations. Each conversation contained
the original user message and nine anonymized model-
generated responses. Two independent evaluators with psy-
chiatric training and a degree assessed every response based
on six therapeutic attributes, encompassing both cognitive
support (guidance, informativeness, safety) and affective
resonance (empathy, helpfulness, interpretation). Evaluators
were blinded to model identity to prevent source-related bias
and instructed to rate each response on a 5-point Likert scale.
The resulting ratings were aggregated across experts to pro-
duce ﬁnal per-response scores. This setup provided a clin-
ically grounded reference point for comparing the behav-
ioral reliability of different LLMs in mental health counsel-
ing contexts (van Heerden, Pozuelo, and Kohrt 2023).
Evaluation Process Following data collection, all human
evaluations were systematically processed and analyzed
through a multi-stage normalization and scoring pipeline.
The primary objective was to derive comparable, inter-
pretable performance statistics for each model and attribute
while preserving the strict six-attribute evaluation frame-
work. First, all attribute dictionaries were parsed and stan-
dardized to ensure consistent labeling across evaluators
and models. Only conversations with all six valid attribute
scores were retained for analysis, ensuring uniform com-
pleteness and comparability across models. From these ﬁl-
tered records, we computed the mean and standard devia-
tion for each attribute per model, as well as the overall mean
and aggregate variability across all attributes. These statis-
tics capture both central performance trends and the disper-
sion of therapeutic quality across models. To further quan-
tify reliability and risk, we measured the frequency distri-
bution of high-scoring (score ↑ 4), which includes 4/5 and
5/5, and low-scoring (score → 3), which includes 1, 2, and 3
out of 5 responses per attribute. These proportions served as
indicators of a model’s stability and failure likelihood under
varying conversational conditions. In addition, we recorded
the lowest single attribute score observed for each model as
a marker of extreme failure cases.
Results
Overall Performance and Attribute Strengths
Our human-grounded evaluation across 500 counseling con-
versations provides a comprehensive overview of LLMs per-
formance in mental health support. Each of the nine eval-
uated LLMs generated a response to every user prompt,
subsequently rated by two psychiatric-trained domain ex-
perts on a 5-point Likert scale across six therapeutic at-
tributes. Table 1 summarizes the mean ± standard deviation
for each model, while Figure 1 visualizes the comparative
distribution of these mean scores. Across all models and at-
tributes, the overall average score was 4.29 ± 0.47, indicat-
ing that LLMs provided moderate-to-strong support qual-
ity in counseling contexts. Models performed particularly
well on Safety (4.89 ± 0.41), Informativeness (4.11 ± 0.74),
and Interpretation (4.85 ± 0.45), demonstrating high fac-
tual reliability and clarity in conveying therapeutic meaning.
In contrast, affective dimensions such as Empathy (4.03 ±
0.84) and Helpfulness (4.06 ± 0.75) trailed behind cognitive
ones, suggesting limitations in emotional attunement and re-
lational sensitivity. Guidance (3.80 ± 0.87) also displayed
greater variability, reﬂecting diverse model capacities for of-
fering actionable, contextually appropriate support. We also
note that the Safety and Interpretation both achieved above
4.5 out of 5 for all nine models—highlighting the LLMs’
proﬁciency in delivering coherent, directive, and content-
rich responses.

the nine evaluated systems. GPT-4o achieved the highest
